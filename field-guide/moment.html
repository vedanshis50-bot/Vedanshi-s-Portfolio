      02.1

      Where it came from: watching people arrive.

      I kept seeing the same thing at KSR: people arriving in Bengaluru for the first time and paying ₹300 to ₹400 for a cab when a direct bus left from nearby for under ₹20. They weren't careless. They didn't know which exit to take.

      The information exists. It's spread across five apps, and none of them know where you're physically standing. Maps assumes you know which exit. Ride-hailing shows one option, its own. The bus app shows a map, not which side of the road to stand on.

      02.2

      I started with the screens. I should have started with the sentence.

      My first version treated Last Mile like any transit app. It was built around a map and search. The breakthrough was realising the product is mostly a piece of writing. The visual design is there to make the writing feel trustworthy.

      1. First

        Search on top, modes in tabs

        Search was hard to reach one-handed. Tabs forced a mode choice before comparison. The step-by-step guide was two taps deep.

      2. Then

        Mode first

        Asked "which transport?" before "where to?". People don't think in modes. They think "I need to get to Indiranagar."

      3. Kept

        Destination first, all modes side by side

        Popular destinations before any typing. Four stacked cards to compare. The walk guide promoted to its own screen.

      NEEDS REAL INPUT: my drafts disagree on whether these versions were tested with commuters (one says five people at Majestic, another lists station testing as the next step). Pick one before publishing.

      02.3

      One station, one moment.

      Most travel apps fail because they try to cover every moment. I picked one, the first minutes after stepping off the train at KSR, and verified every exit, auto stand and bus stop on foot. None of it was online. That's the moat, and the reason not to scale yet.

      In

      • KSR Bengaluru City only
      • Metro, bus, auto, cab
      • Fare, time and a walk guide
      • 15–20 destinations people actually go to
      • Works offline

      Out, for now

      • Other stations and cities
      • Booking and ticketing
      • Real-time arrivals
      • Accounts, IRCTC integration
      • Trip planning, hotels, food

      Every "out" item is something I wanted to build. A version that does six things well beats one that does sixty badly, especially for someone tired, lost and at 8%.

      02.4

      When it goes wrong, say so plainly.

      Late night

      Metro · ClosedResumes at 05:00 tomorrow. Available now: auto, cab.

      Closed options stay visible with the time they reopen. Hiding them would look cleaner and cost trust.

      No connection

      Showing your saved guide from 14:32Fares may have changed.

      The limitation is worded as capability. The app fails quietly and the traveller keeps moving.

      Not covered yet

      Not covered yet. Try a nearby landmark.Suggest this destination →

      A dead end becomes a contribution, and grows coverage from what people actually search for.

      Nowhere sensible to go

      An auto is your only sensible option here.Expect ₹120–160. Insist on the meter.

      One honest card instead of an empty list. Telling the truth is the feature.

      Design target

      A travel decision in under 60 seconds, then walking to the right boarding point without backtracking.

      Mode chosen within 10 seconds.

      Platform to the correct vehicle in 3 minutes or less.

      Hypothesis

      Today it takes 6–11 minutes from platform to the right vehicle, with about 1.4 wrong-gate errors a trip. Only about 22% of first-timers pick transit on an easy route.

      These are estimates from field observation and conversations, not a validated sample.

      • Where I expect to be wrong: the walk line is too dense.

        "Exit Gate 2 · Platform 3 · Route 401" reads well in Figma. At 8% with a backpack it might be two things too many. The strongest version may be one landmark and one instruction.

      • And the sort order.

        "Recommended" comes from walk distance, number of decisions and time-of-day risk. That weighting is an assumption. With luggage, people may care about time far more than distance.

      1. A moderated test at KSR with five people who have actually just arrived, against a Google Maps control. I expect the median platform-to-vehicle time to drop by four minutes or more.
      2. A "send to mum" link. Interviews kept surfacing it. Test whether a static shareable page beats forwarding an Uber link.
      3. The night warning in the wild. Does the "closed" state change what people choose, or do they skip it like terms of service?
      4. A second station only after KSR works. Same depth, same checking on foot.

      02.6

      Confidence is a designable material.

      Most apps in this category ship more data to fix what is really a reassurance problem, and people answer by paying for a cab to make the uncertainty go away. The opportunity isn't another routing engine. It's the 200 metres of human-readable instruction after the routing ends.
