The real problem

Nobody rejected the idea. It just never got tested.

This is how most ideas travel today — six stages, and the same unproven assumption riding along in all of them.

  1. Idea“I think this could be something.”
  2. Belief“Sounds good, let's do it.”
  3. Commitment“We should build this.”
  4. Investment“We've already invested.”
  5. Launch“Finally — it's ready.”
  6. Reality“Nobody wanted this.”
The real loss isn't the idea failing. It's the wasted resources, misaligned teams, missed opportunities and lost trust it takes down with it.

What if ideas could fight back?

The problem

We don't fail because we lack ideas. We fail because we fall in love with them.

The most dangerous moment in product development isn't when an idea fails. It's when everyone starts agreeing too quickly. The unanswered questions, hidden assumptions and overlooked risks quietly survive every review until they become expensive mistakes.

What it costs when nobody argues back

Two real products, two untested assumptions.

Not hypothetical risk — two well-documented, public examples of the exact failure mode Boardroom AI is designed to catch earlier.

Google+

Social network · shut down after 8 years

Google's answer to Facebook launched on a simple internal logic: bundle a social layer into Gmail, YouTube and Search, and adoption would follow. Millions of accounts were created — but signups aren't the same as people wanting to be there.

<5 secaverage length of 90% of user sessions, by the platform's own later admission

The assumption never challenged: scale was mistaken for validation. Nobody was in the room to ask for the difference.

Quibi

Short-form mobile streaming · shut down 6 months after launch

Quibi bet everything on one belief: that people wanted premium, 10-minute shows built only for their commute — watched between a coffee run and a train ride. Leadership had pedigree, Hollywood talent and total conviction. What it never had was a room where someone asked out loud: what if people simply stay on the apps they already open?

$1.75Braised, returned, gone within 6 months

The assumption never challenged: nobody in the room was assigned to argue against the core bet — only to help it launch faster.

The reframe

Before you build, make the idea defend itself.

Every product decision starts as an assumption. Untested, it can survive months of meetings before it becomes an expensive mistake. What if every idea had to prove who it's for, why now, and what could go wrong — before it got a single hour of work?

Boardroom AI helps teams challenge ideas before they invest time, money and development effort in them.

Meet the boardroom

So I built a room of six perspectives.

Different minds, different incentives. One goal: build what actually matters. Their value isn't in six separate answers — it's in what happens when the answers disagree.

CEO

Vision and priority

  • Is this a great business?
  • Can we win this market?
  • Will this still matter in three years?
Investor

ROI and risk

  • What is the upside?
  • What are we underestimating?
  • Why now and not later?
Engineer

Feasibility and technical truth

  • Can we build this reliably?
  • What are the technical risks?
  • What will this cost us later?
UX Researcher

User trust

  • What do users truly need?
  • What did we not validate?
  • What will surprise us later?
Customer

Adoption and trust

  • Will this make my life better?
  • What would make me unhappy?
  • Why should I choose this?
Strategist

The long game

  • Does this create a real edge?
  • How does this fit our strategy?
  • What are we saying no to?

An idea doesn't get six opinions. It gets cross-examined. Example of the kind of exchange the room is designed to produce:

  • CEO“This could be a strong opportunity.”
  • Engineer“The architecture doesn't support that scale.”
  • Customer“I'm not convinced anyone needs it.”
  • Investor“What's the evidence?”
  • Strategist“If we build this, what are we not building?”

The interaction

One decision, start to verdict.

The interaction isn't one screen, it's a sequence. Here's the path a founder walks, and what each state is designed to make them feel.

01Entry
States one decision in a single field — not a product brief, one sentence.
Designed to feelCommitment — name the real question.
02Board assembles
Watches the six members take their seats, one by one.
Designed to feelSlight intimidation — they are prepared.
03Debate
Watches positions move across the room and clash.
Designed to feelTension.
04Cross-examination
Sees a claim challenged with a real comparable, not just an opinion.
Designed to feelPressure — the assumption is exposed.
05The turn
Watches the room converge as the original question gets reframed.
Designed to feelClarity forming.
06The verdict
Reads the ruling, the vote, and the minority opinion.
Designed to feelClarity — a ruling, not “feedback”.
07Homework
Gets specific things to prove before the next session — not applause.
Designed to feelObligation — a next step.

The design challenge

How do you design disagreement?

The hard part was never making six AI personas look attractive. It was the information architecture of disagreement — deciding what a founder sees, what stays hidden, and when tension turns into noise instead of clarity.

Show what helps a decision, not everything the model says

Raw AI output isn't the interface. The room only surfaces what moves a founder from opinion to evidence.

Disagreement needs a shape, not a wall of text

Six people talking at once is noise. A claim and the comparable that challenges it is a decision aid.

A verdict, not a feedback dump

What we agree on, what we don't, the biggest risk, the minority opinion, what to prove next — that's more useful than “feasible.”

Why not just ask one AI?

One question gets one general answer. Boardroom AI trades that for structured disagreement — the value isn't “AI knows more,” it's that the idea has to survive being cross-examined.

The honest version

The design was never about visuals.

I came in thinking the hard part was making six AI personas look good. It wasn't. The hard part was the information architecture of disagreement: deciding what to show, what to hide, and how to make a founder feel a split without explaining it to them.

That's the part I'd carry into any product — figure out what the interface should make someone feel before deciding what it should look like.
